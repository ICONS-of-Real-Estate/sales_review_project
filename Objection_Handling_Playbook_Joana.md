## 1. Ends the pitch with an opinion question instead of a direct ask

**Real examples:** Will Salinas (05/08) — closed a complete pricing presentation with "for you and your team, what do you feel that you wanted to go with?"; Ryan Welch (05/08) — "we only have a couple of minutes for you to tell me what do you think about it?"; Milton Webster (12/08) — "But yeah, what do you think?", minutes after Milton himself raised launch timelines, a direct buying signal; Heather Gorney (12/08) — "So tell me, what do you think?", with no direct ask anywhere else in the call.

**Why it happens:** An opinion question feels softer and less confrontational than a direct ask, but it hands the prospect an easy, non-committal exit instead of a real decision point.

**Technique — Replace the opinion question with a specific, answerable ask.** Every one of these calls had already done the hard work (full pricing walkthrough, discovery, rapport) — the miss is entirely in the final sentence. An either/or or conditional close gives the prospect something concrete to say yes or no to, instead of an open invitation to think about it.

**Suggested response (adapt to the specific package/timeline discussed):**
> "Which package do you want to start with — the $897 weekly or the $597 biweekly?"

or, when a real buying signal or timeline has already come up:
> "When [the thing they're waiting on] settles, which package do you want to start with — shall we pencil in the launchpad for that month?"

**Coaching note:** a trial-close question ("does that sound good?", "what do you think?") is not a real ask, even when everything before it in the call went well — this is exactly Failure Mode 1 from the shared rubric (`Phase2_CallGradingSOP.md` §3).

## 2. Accepts a stall or deferral as final instead of converting it into a commitment

**Real examples:** Peg Walsh (06/08) — responded "Perfect. Perfect. That's great" to "I'm going to think about it... I could do it down the road, I think"; Kelli Eggen (11/08) — "Of course. Of course," to "I'm not ready to commit yet... definitely want to do this," despite Kelli having already called the $597 biweekly package "not bad"; Christina Tokar (13/08) — accepted "If we start this, we'll start it in September" and simply scheduled a September follow-up; Manny Chamizo III (11/08) — after Manny said "let's move forward," sent a booking link to use "whenever" instead of locking a specific time; Jacqueline Coleman (14/08) — "It's not a no" per Joana's own read, but no explicit ask was ever made before ending the call.

**Why it happens:** These are engaged, warm prospects — the miss isn't losing the lead, it's leaving real momentum sitting open-ended instead of turning it into a dated or conditional commitment while interest is highest.

**Technique — Convert every stall into a conditional or dated close on the spot.** In all five calls, Joana had already earned a real signal (a stated timeline, a positive reaction to pricing, a verbal "let's move forward") — the fix is asking one more question that turns that signal into a commitment, rather than accepting it as a natural stopping point.

**Suggested response (adapt to what was actually said):**
> "Since September works, shall we get the launch kit done now so your first episode is ready the week you're back?"

> "If your team is on board at 10am, shall we get you started on the weekly package?"

**Coaching note:** booking a follow-up is not the same as closing — a follow-up with nothing concrete attached to it is exactly how a warm lead goes cold.

## 3. A real objection gets surfaced but answered with nothing concrete

**Real examples:** Shannon Driessen (06/08) — "I really don't have the budget for it right now" met with "Perfect. Shannon, I'm going to send you these," despite Joana having already set up both the sponsor cost-offset math and a sub-$500 tier earlier in the same call; Mark Vincent Fansler (07/08) — pressed three separate times for in-studio equipment specifics, told "I am not the person to tell you as well... but our team will be making sure that you have everything that you need," despite this being the one blocker between him and a booked order; Douglas Rill (13/08) — raised "money's a little bit short" and floated "something on the pricing," and the call drifted back to small talk with no answer; Joseph Bradley (20/08) — named the DIY alternative ("I could just create a conversation through Zoom and then I can just post it") and got "You can, but" — the thought was never finished.

**Why it happens:** The objection gets acknowledged, but the concrete tool to resolve it (a number, a mechanism, a specific answer) either isn't used even when it's already on the table (Shannon), or the rep genuinely doesn't have it ready (Mark's equipment specs).

**Technique — Never let an acknowledged objection go unanswered.** If a concrete resolution already exists (a pricing tier, a cost-offset mechanism), use it the moment the objection lands, not earlier in the call and not never. If the answer requires expertise the rep doesn't have (equipment specs), have that answer ready before the call, not deferred to "our team will handle it."

**Suggested response (adapt to the actual objection and tool available):**
> "Totally hear you on budget — remember the sponsor can offset [$X] of the monthly fee, and there's also a [sub-$500 tier] if that's a better fit to start. Which of those works better for you?"

**Coaching note:** this overlaps with the shared rubric's Failure Mode 2 (objections uncovered but not overcome) — surfacing the concern is only half the job.

## 4. No quantified proof point ready when capability or results get challenged

**Real examples:** Tim Saeland (20/08) — asked for best/worst client examples right after saying it "sounds affordable," got "I will ask that to, to my co-founder and I will send you in a bit"; Jess Provencher (20/08) — asked what results were realistic while holding low review counts, got a reframe about vanity metrics but no quantified client result to anchor it; Jason Pietruszka (12/08, partial credit — did advance the calendar correctly) — asked whether cross-zip-code sourcing was feasible, got "I mean, that's totally something we can do. Yeah, yeah, for sure" with no evidence behind it.

**Why it happens:** These are legitimate diligence questions from engaged prospects, and enthusiasm alone doesn't resolve a "prove it" moment — especially when the prospect is already leaning toward yes.

**Technique — Walk into every call with one named, quantified client result ready to deliver live.** Deferring a proof request ("I'll ask my co-founder and send it over") loses the moment when the prospect is most receptive; the answer needs to be immediate and specific — a name, a number, a mechanism.

**Suggested response:**
> "Actually, [client name] was in a really similar spot — [specific quantified result]. That's the kind of outcome we're aiming for with you too."

**Coaching note:** Jason's call is the closest to a model answer in this batch — he still advanced to booking a four-person call with Tomás despite the missed proof point. That instinct (advance anyway) is right; pairing it with a real proof point would have made it a clean 5 instead of a 4.

## Suggested practice format (for the Kris/Tomás session with Joana)

1. Read one real prospect line from the list above (not the suggested response) and have Joana respond live, cold.
2. Compare against the suggested response — not to script-memorize it, but to check the response hits the same shape: a specific, answerable ask or a concrete resolution, never an opinion question or an accepted stall.
3. Close every drill by asking Joana to practice converting a stated timeline or positive signal into a dated commitment on the spot — patterns #1 and #2 together account for 9 of the 16 real cases in this batch, by far the single biggest lever available.
