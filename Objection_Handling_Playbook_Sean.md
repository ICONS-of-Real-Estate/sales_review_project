## 1. Budget / needs money freed up first

**Real examples:** William Schlunaker ("$300 a month for nothing" on one failed vendor, "$600 a month for the two of those... it's just a matter of how much money I have after I cancel"), Roxy Miles ("it really depends on like the investment, like long-term, cuz I have other marketing things that I'm working on" — raised twice), Marcus Jackson ("out of my range, so"), Parisa Daily ("that price point, that's nearly $1,000 per month, that's an extra expense... I just want to take some time to mull it over"), Tyrone Mingo ("I'm waiting for the closing, so once I get the closing, then I'll be ready"), Justin Abel ("my car is about to die, I got a lot of financial crap going on"), Lei McDonald Pt.2 (needing sponsorships to afford it).

**Why it happens:** Real estate agents are largely 1099 commission-only — cash flow is genuinely lumpy, and a recurring monthly cost competes directly against other active marketing spend or a pending closing.

**Technique — Get the real number before discounting.** In every single one of these calls, Sean's response was a discount (Marcus: $1,500 → $934) or a generic anecdote (Roxy: a 20-year-old personal ad-budget story with no numbers tied to her actual business) — never the lead's own numbers. Discovery should surface what they're already spending before price ever comes up, so the ROI comparison is concrete rather than generic.

**Suggested response:**
> "What's the number one thing you're doing for marketing right now, and roughly what does that cost you a month? [...] So you're already spending $X on that — this replaces or stacks on top of it, and here's what it's produced for someone in a similar spot: [matching case study]. One closing pays for a year or more of this." If the blocker is genuinely cash-flow timing rather than skepticism, offer the actual mechanism — start on the lower tier now, or use the sponsorship program to offset cost from month one — with a specific date for when the higher tier kicks in.

## 2. Needs a third party's approval (partner, spouse, coach, investor)

**Real examples:** Elijah Castelli Pt.1 ("I want to make sure that I got Lindsay's approval on it... there'll be a little bit of a fight on it"), Steve Robe ("I got to run it by Tom... he watches my P&L, and he'll freak out"), William Schlunaker ("I'd like to go talk to him first and see what he's willing to give me"), Kodie Smiley (a silent investor, her ex-husband), Justin Abel (a business partner).

**Why it happens:** Many of these leads run their business jointly (a spouse, a coach who reviews the books, a team's managing broker) — the purchase decision genuinely isn't theirs alone to make.

**Technique — Ask who else is involved *before* pitching, not after the close attempt.** Elijah's call is the clearest miss: Sean never asked who else needed to be comfortable with the decision, so it surfaced only at the very end with no plan, and Sean let Elijah dictate an open-ended reschedule with himself instead of routing to Tomás. Steve Robe's call is the model to copy: Sean proactively invited the coach onto the next call.

**Suggested response:**
> "Who else needs to be comfortable with this before you move forward?" — asked during discovery. Once you know there's a co-decision-maker: either offer to loop them into this call or a quick follow-up right now, or give the lead a specific one-page summary with the real numbers just discussed to bring to that person. Either way, lock a specific date and time for the follow-up before ending the call — never "I'll get back to you."

## 3. Timing / wants to think it over

**Real examples:** Nicole Beauchamp Pt.2 ("I would have to give it some thought... that's a lot for thought"), Parisa Daily ("It's always an investment when you're spending money towards your business... I just want to take some time to mull it over"), Marcus Jackson ("do a little bit more serious homework and due diligence"), Justin Abel ("I'm not ready to flip the switch"), Tyrone Mingo (waiting on a closing).

**Why it happens:** Sometimes a genuine need for more information; sometimes a polite way of not naming the real objection (usually budget or a co-decision-maker) directly.

**Technique — Isolate before accepting.** Parisa Daily's call is the one place in this whole batch Sean asked the right diagnostic question: "is it the price, the timing, the information?" That's the model — copy it every time instead of accepting "let me think about it" at face value (as happened with Nicole Beauchamp, where Sean just pivoted straight to booking Tomás).

**Suggested response:**
> "What specifically do you need to think through — is it the investment, the timing, or something about how this works?" Whatever they name, answer that thing concretely on the spot using the matching technique above, then get a specific day/time locked before hanging up.

## 4. ROI / proof / metrics skepticism

**Real examples:** Kodie Smiley ("any... averages of subscribers... any sort of metrics that you guys are watching as far as their growth?" — answered "I'm sure that there are... Tomas will be able to show you on our next Zoom"), Elijah Castelli Pt.1 ("How many subscribers would we be like looking to get?" — answered with a philosophy statement instead of a number).

**Why it happens:** A reasonable, sophisticated question from a lead deciding whether the mechanism actually works, not just whether the pitch sounds good.

**Technique — Know the real numbers cold; don't punt to Tomás.** Deferring a metrics question to the next call reads as not knowing your own product. Sean has real stats available (89% of clients see results in the first month, 92% renewal rate per other parts of the pitch) — those should answer this exact question live, paired with a specific case study.

**Suggested response:**
> "89% of clients see results in the very first month, and our renewal rate is 92% — here's a specific example close to your situation: [case study]." If a specific number genuinely isn't known cold, commit to getting it back same-day rather than waiting for a whole second call.

## 5. Content or personal-brand fit concern

**Real examples:** Nicole Beauchamp Pt.2 (outsourcing booking/coordination clashes with her high-touch personal brand — answered "I agree, we'll comply" with no mechanism), Marcus Jackson (mixing existing home-tour content with new educational content — "it'll be very smooth," no example), Lei McDonald Pt.2 (needing a "studio" look — this one Sean handled well, with a real number: ~$50 for a mic, lighting, and a camera).

**Why it happens:** Established agents already have a content style and audience expectation; a generic production process can feel like a threat to that, not an addition.

**Technique — Get specific, not reassuring.** Use the Lei McDonald answer as the template. For a brand-voice concern, name a real client who kept full creative control; for a content-mixing concern, describe the actual production step where existing content gets folded in.

**Suggested response:**
> For brand fit: "[Name] kept full script review and creative control the whole way through — here's exactly how that worked." For content mixing: describe the specific production workflow step, or commit to a same-day follow-up with a real example if you don't have a clean answer ready.

## 6. Compliance / regulatory concern

**Real example:** Lei McDonald Pt.2 — "I think that mortgage brokers are not allowed to do that... They cannot pay" (referring to sponsorship payments). Sean: "I'm not particularly sure of the rules... every state has different rules and stuff like that" — and dropped it.

**Why it happens:** Mortgage brokers and some other licensees operate under real regulatory restrictions on referral fees and payments — a legitimate concern, not a stall.

**Technique — Never leave a compliance question unanswered on the call.** If you don't know, say so and commit to a real deadline rather than guessing or dropping it.

**Suggested response:**
> "That's a fair question I want to get exactly right for you rather than guess — let me get our compliance answer and have it back to you by [specific day], and let's put 15 minutes on the calendar then to close the loop." An unresolved compliance question is a legitimate reason a deal stalls forever if nobody follows up.

## 7. Minor pricing friction (a fee, not the core price)

**Real example:** Elijah Castelli Pt.2 — Lindsay: "Friday, man. That hurts, that $76" (the card processing fee). Sean: "we can't get around it... it's not that much... you'll get sponsors anyway." Didn't block this sale, but was still a deflection rather than a real answer.

**Why it happens:** A last-second, small line-item that stands out because it appears right at the moment of payment, not because it's actually significant.

**Technique — State it as a fact with a number, not an apology.** Don't undersell a real answer with a shrug.

**Suggested response:**
> "That's the standard card processing rate, not a markup we're adding — and once your first sponsor comes on, it's a rounding error against what they're covering."

## 8. Scheduling / logistics request

**Real example:** Steve Robe — wanted to batch-record several episodes at once around his travel schedule. Sean's answer: "tell our team, 'I want to do four podcasts on Wednesday,' and you do them back to back," plus mentioned the Riverside remote-recording app.

**This is the one objection in the batch that needs no fix — it's the standard the rest of this playbook is aiming for:** specific mechanism, not a vague reassurance.

## 9. Comparing against a competitor / wants to shop around

**Real example:** Deme Mekras (4/27 call) — "the only new information... I actually just heard the ad, frankly, on Friday" (a competing real-estate podcast service's iHeartRadio ad), and he explicitly says he still needs to "click on one of these or two of these to hear it" before deciding.

**Why it happens:** A sophisticated, already-warm lead doing real due diligence, not a brush-off — he'd already done the reference call Sean asked for and was actively comparing options.

**Technique — Get the specific competitor and answer with real numbers, not superiority claims.** Sean's response mixed some good material (a named case study, a stated competitor price point, a retention stat) with unearned bluster ("I can tell you without even looking," "night and day difference guaranteed" — while also saying "I don't even know what they're offering"). Never claim to already know you're better than something you haven't looked at.

**Suggested response:**
> "Which one — I want to actually look at what they offer so I'm comparing apples to apples, not just telling you we're better." Then use real numbers already in hand (price, retention rate, a matching case study) against the *specific* thing they heard, and re-ask for the close once it's answered — don't let the comparison research become the reason the call ends without a next step.

## 10. Trust / a prior bad experience with a similar vendor

**Real example:** Bently Perry — the lead's mother, who was paying, raised it directly: "My mom got billed $25,000. They did nothing," right after asking, "Do you have a contract that guarantees the work... that we can hold you guys accountable to?" Sean said nothing in response to the $25k story; the lead talked herself past it before he re-engaged.

**Why it happens:** Real estate marketing has a real history of expensive vendors that under-deliver — this is a rational fear from someone who's been burned before, not a stall.

**Technique — Never let a "we got burned before" story pass without a direct response.** A contract's existence isn't the answer to a fear about accountability — name the actual mechanism (a specific guarantee, a milestone check-in, a named point of contact) and, if a case study is available, a client who had the same fear.

**Suggested response:**
> "I hear why that's scary — here's exactly what makes this different: [specific guarantee/check-in mechanism], and here's someone who had that same worry before working with us: [case study]." Answer it in the moment it's raised — don't let it go unaddressed just because the lead moves on themselves.

## Cross-cutting patterns (v2 — bigger than any single objection type)

Two patterns showed up across the 18/08 batch more consistently than any individual objection above, and both compound the "objections_overcome and discovery_adequate both false" finding from v1:

**A. Almost never asks the lead to state their own numeric goal.** Ross Seligman, Debra J. Cook, Amelia Breslin, Michael Blevins, Julio Lopez, Skyler Hynes, and both Deme Mekras calls all show the same gap: Sean pitches generic value ("increase your production," "establishes you as the authority") without ever asking the lead to name a number (a target GCI, a transaction count, a specific growth goal). Without that number, there's nothing lead-specific to fall back on the moment a real objection surfaces — which is exactly when Sean defaults to generic case studies or bluster instead of a tailored answer. **Add this as a required discovery question on every call, before the pitch, not just when a call permits it:** *"What's your production goal for this year — a number, not a feeling?"*

**B. Gets partial progress, then stops pushing instead of locking down the rest.** Ward Frederick (4/23) and Bently Perry both show this: Sean secures *something* (a verbal yes, a launch-pad payment) and then folds — no smaller ask when the bigger one is declined, no second call with Tomás for the remaining decision, a follow-up booked with himself instead. This is a different failure than "no close ask" (he did ask) — it's stopping the moment there's *any* win instead of finishing the job. **Drill this explicitly:** once you get a partial yes, the very next sentence should either ask for a smaller concrete commitment or say "let's get Tomás on a specific date to lock in the rest" — never just "no worries, talk soon."

## What good already looks like

Three moments across this batch are worth holding up as the standard:

- **Lei McDonald Pt.2, 8/14** — the gear/studio-cost objection, answered with a real number ("spent like 50 bucks... microphone, some lighting, and a camera").
- **Steve Robe, 7/21** — the batch-recording logistics request, answered with a concrete production mechanism and a named tool (Riverside).
- **Elijah Castelli Pt.2, 7/24 — the only closed sale in the v1 batch.** Elijah's stated goal was explicit and specific ("we really want to grow this thing to a number one in the state of Tennessee"), and Sean tied the recommended package directly to it with a real analogy (a probate officer who went from locally-known to nationally-known using the same mechanism), rather than a generic pitch. That specific goal-to-offer tie is what separated this call from the other 11, even though its own objection handling (the card-fee complaint) and discovery (a recap call, no new numbers) were no better than the rest of the batch.
- **Deme Mekras, 5/1 (v2) — a clean, model close, top score of the whole project so far.** The one real objection (a pricing discrepancy) got a concrete, honest resolution (Sean looked it up and honored the lead's original pre-increase quote), the close ask was direct and led to an actual $1,899 charge, and the pitch was tied to specifics of Dean's own business (his existing YouTube channel, his boutique-to-commercial growth goal, his actual vacation schedule) rather than anything generic. This is the standard both cross-cutting patterns above are asking Sean to hit on every call, not just this one.

The common thread: every one of these moments used a real number, a named example, or a specific mechanism — exactly what was missing everywhere else in both batches.

## Suggested practice format (for the Kris/Tomás session with Sean)

1. Read one real prospect quote from the list above (not the suggested response) and have Sean respond live, cold.
2. Compare against the suggested response — not to script-memorize it, but to check the response hits the same shape: get the real number or name a real example, never agreement-only, a discount-only, or a deflection to "Tomás will answer that."
3. Close every drill by practicing the "who else is involved in this decision?" discovery question from objection #2 and the "what specifically do you need to think through?" isolation question from objection #3 — both are cheap to add to every call and directly address the two failure modes (weak discovery, accepting a vague stall) present in all 12 v1 calls reviewed.
4. **(v2 addition)** Drill the numeric-goal question from Cross-cutting pattern A ("what's your production goal — a number, not a feeling?") at the very start of a mock discovery, before any pitch language. Then run a "partial yes" role-play for pattern B: feed Sean a scripted verbal yes or a small payment mid-drill and have him practice the very next sentence — it should be a smaller concrete ask or a specific Tomás booking, never "no worries, talk soon."
