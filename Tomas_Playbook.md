# Tomás Playbook — What Other Reps Should Copy

> Companion to `Phase2_CallGradingSOP.md` and the rep-specific objection playbooks
> (`Objection_Handling_Playbook.md` for Bens, `Objection_Handling_Playbook_Sean.md`
> for Sean). Those documents are mostly "here's the mistake, here's the fix."
> This one runs the other direction: Tomás is the team's best closer, so this is
> a direct extraction of what he actually does — on real calls, in his own words —
> that the other reps should be trained to copy. Tomás should review and edit this
> before it's used in a team training session.

## How this is maintained

Built from a close read of **22 real Tomás call transcripts** (14 of his own
first-touch Sales Calls, 8 second/closing calls picking up after Sean or Bens),
sampled deliberately to include leads who had already raised a known objection
with another rep before reaching Tomás — so we could check whether he
pre-empts it. Read 21/08/2026. Like the other playbooks, this is a **living
document** — append new patterns as future call batches get reviewed, and
flag anything Tomás disagrees with rather than deleting it silently.

**Data-quality note:** 3 of the 22 sampled transcripts were not usable and
are excluded from the findings below — flag these to whoever owns the
transcription pipeline rather than assuming they contain nothing useful:
- *Danny Rodriguez – 2nd Sales Call* and *Stella Shalamova – 3rd Sales Call*:
  both transcripts are entirely `[BLANK_AUDIO]` tokens — failed
  transcriptions, no speech captured.
- *Phuong Phan Sales Call*: transcribes cleanly for roughly the first third,
  then degenerates into one line repeated ~1,100 times. Everything after

## Part 1 — The core process: solve the problem before the objection exists

This is the single biggest thing to copy, and it's exactly the SOP's own
principle in Section 3 — *"objection prevention beats objection handling"* —
made concrete. Across the calls reviewed, Tomás's strongest calls all follow
the same four-step shape, done **before** the lead has objected to anything:

1. **Ask, or re-verify, the lead's actual goal early** — not "are you
   interested in a podcast" but "what are you actually trying to grow /
   fix / get more of." On second calls, don't assume the first rep's notes
   are enough — if they're thin, re-run this live.
2. **Mirror the lead's own words back**, almost verbatim, when you introduce
   the offer.
3. **Map the mechanism to that specific goal** — not "a podcast gets you
   exposure," but the actual named feature (guest-avatar recruiting,
   guesting instead of hosting, probate-specific positioning) that answers
   what they just said.
4. **Back it with something concrete** — a named past client, a real dollar
   figure, or a live on-screen demo — not a general reassurance.

**The clearest example — Ward Frederick.** Ward opens by stating he doesn't
care about vanity metrics, only revenue: *"I only care whether it makes me
money... most of my business comes from repeat and referrals."* Before
raising any objection, Tomás mirrors it back almost word for word: *"I'm
glad you say that because we're not about the vanity as well... we only do
podcasts that are directly helping your business grow,"* then maps the offer
to Ward's specific niche (large acreage/timberland/cell-tower deals) as a
positioning play. This is the model to teach from.

**Other strong examples:**
- **Gary Lanham** — Tomás draws out Gary's pivot toward becoming a probate
  specialist, then answers it with a named, numbered case study before any
  pushback: *"Matias... closed five million dollars worth of property from
  the podcast... three transactions... all came from referrals from the
  guests' connections."*
- **Jason Flesher** — Jason states he's camera-shy. Tomás answers with
  Jason's **own live Instagram data** (posts with his face get 60+ likes vs.
  11–20 without) as personalized proof the format still works for him —
  more persuasive than any generic reassurance because it's the lead's own
  numbers.
- **Phil Nykamp** — closed. Tomás re-runs discovery himself on a second call
  rather than trusting Sean's notes, learns Phil's business is 100%
  referral/community-based, and reframes the whole offer around it:
  *"we call them community heroes... we never push people to just do a
  podcast about real estate, because you would have very limited
  viewership... the idea is to always sprinkle both real estate and
  community in the same podcast."*
- **Karlene and Curtis Grimes** — ties the offer to their stated recruiting
  goal with a named mechanism: *"we'll match your ideal recruit with your
  ideal guest,"* plus a recent proof point ("three agents... recruited from
  different podcasts" last month).
- **The live "dead competitor" search** (used near-identically on **Thom
  Tillier** and **Phuong Phan**) — Tomás searches "[lead's city] real estate
  podcast" live on screen and shows the top result hasn't posted in over a
  year, proving the content is evergreen and the competitive bar is low.
  This is cheap, repeatable, and lets the lead see the proof rather than
  just hear it — worth teaching as its own drill.

**What happens without this — the counter-examples:**
- **Michael Abraham** — Tomás never asks a real discovery question about
  Michael's business goals before floating "you should start your own
  podcast" at the very end of the call. When Michael pushes back ("not
  right now... maybe Q4"), Tomás has nothing specific to counter with and
  just pivots to a different ask. No discovery → no framing → no answer to
  the objection.
- **Calvin Wong** — the goal-to-offer connection only gets made *after*
  Calvin has already said "I don't plan to have a podcast this year" twice.
  By then the pitch reads as a generic recovery attempt, not a targeted
  answer, and the call ends without Calvin actually retracting the "no."
- **Rob Commodari** — the call turns into a feature-by-feature comparison
  against Rob's existing $600/mo editor (thumbnails, carousels, vertical
  video). Tomás matches features instead of re-anchoring on outcome
  (revenue, authority, time saved) the way he does in the Ward/Gary/Jason
  calls — a spec war instead of a value argument.
- **Katie Uei — a useful exception, not a failure.** Katie has no stated
  marketing problem, so Tomás doesn't manufacture one: *"you're doing
  great... you don't need a podcast."* Worth teaching too — the point of
  Part 1 isn't "always pitch," it's "only pitch a solution to a problem the
  lead actually has."

## Part 2 — Objection handling, by type

### 1. "I already have this" (own podcast, in-house production, a VA/Claude workflow)

**Technique — don't compete with it, sell the adjacent thing.**
- **Jeff Goodman** already ran his own podcast and stopped from lack of
  time. Instead of re-selling him on producing another show, Tomás pivots
  to a different, cheaper product that solves the actual constraint (time):
  *"Would you be interested if I can put something together for you to
  guest on podcasts?... You don't need to worry about the editing,
  repurposing, promotion... it's like being a guest on someone else's
  dinner... you don't need to do the dishes."*
- **Bill Gross** runs 3 podcasts fully in-house and isn't convinced it'd be
  cheaper with ICONS. Tomás doesn't argue cost or quality — he reframes the
  entire offer as a partnership/affiliate arrangement instead, with real
  numbers (*"50 percent of the first month payment... up to twenty-five
  hundred... or a recurring... 10 percent"*), matching Bill's actual stated
  ambition (recruiting) rather than his current production setup.
- **Jason Flesher** already uses a VA and Claude for his business — Tomás
  validates rather than treats it as competing ("100% you're going on a
  good route... I'm doing the same myself"), though this is also flagged
  below as a missed step (he never ties the VA/AI time savings back to
  freeing up time *for* the podcast).

### 2. Price / ROI

**Technique — a real number or mechanism when the math can work; an honest "no" when it can't.**
- **Ward Frederick** — skeptical of small audience size. Tomás answers with
  real math: *"if one of them is interested per episode, that's 52 [a
  year]... even if you only close 10% of that, that's five... you can turn
  the ROI on what five deals is worth to you."*
- **Chad Williams** — fatigued by "spend $100 to make $100" marketing.
  Tomás had already anchored ROI earlier in the call: *"you only have to
  sell one house to pay for the podcast for the whole year"* — a standing
  reference point he could fall back on rather than needing a new number
  live.
- **Tennitia Wilson** — the honest counter-example. Her brokerage's real
  numbers leave ~$500/month in margin; she'd already told Bens the price
  felt prohibitive. Tomás doesn't discount or manufacture a bridge: *"I
  would love to tell you that we do it for free... but we don't."* No sale,
  but also no pressure — worth teaching as "some price objections are just
  correct, and forcing it anyway costs trust."

### 3. "Too busy / no bandwidth"

**Technique — proactively narrow the ask to exactly what the lead has to do.**
- **Cory Boldroff**, who'd already flagged limited bandwidth, gets this
  before he can object to the offer on those grounds: *"The only thing that
  you need to worry about is two things... recording... and to nurture and
  capitalize on the relationships... everything else... we do it."*
- **Heather Gill** describes a burnout history with prior content attempts.
  Rather than pushing the full podcast past her stated limit, Tomás offers
  a smaller-commitment wedge product (paid guesting, $87/mo) that fits the
  constraint instead of arguing with it.

### 4. "Does this even fit my niche / my business?"

**Technique — a named, directly analogous case study, not a generic assurance.**
- **Chad Williams** (estate-sale/auction niche) worries the platform won't
  fit his specialty. Tomás answers with a matched case study: a probate
  attorney (Matthias Baker) who became the top podcast in his own niche —
  same shape of concern, same shape of proof.
- **Karlene and Curtis Grimes** get the same treatment for recruiting
  specifically, with a named process (guest-avatar matching) rather than a
  vague "it'll help you grow."

### 5. Skepticism about audience size, vanity metrics, or the platform itself

**Technique — reframe the metric to revenue math, or show live proof.**
- **Ward Frederick**'s "100 people isn't a big audience" gets the 52-episode
  math above.
- **Chad Williams**'s "I don't want a podcast with one view" gets reframed
  directly: *"would you prefer a podcast that has 10,000 views and brings
  you one sale per year, or a podcast that has 200 views and brings you one
  sale per month?"*
- **Thom Tillier / Phuong Phan** get the live dead-competitor search
  described in Part 1 — visual proof beats a verbal claim.

### 6. Stalling — "I need to check with someone / decide something else first"

**This is the weakest spot in Tomás's own game, and worth coaching him on
directly, not just copying.** In several calls he accepts an open-ended
stall rather than converting it into a specific, smaller next step:
- **Ward Frederick** — needs to loop in his son and finish a pending
  brokerage decision. Tomás accepts it passively (*"I wish you the best in
  that decision"*) instead of separating the two decisions or proposing a
  provisional next step.
- **Rob Commodari** — needs 1–2 weeks then goes on vacation. No bridge or
  onboarding-in-the-meantime offer.
- **Gary Lanham** — flags a possible brokerage change; appropriately not
  pushed here, since it's a genuinely bigger decision than the podcast.

**The better version of this exists in the same call set — Chad Williams**:
instead of an open-ended stall, Tomás/Sean lock a firm next-day time
("let's touch base tomorrow evening... 3:30pm") rather than leaving it
vague. **Teach this contrast directly**: a stall should always end with a
specific date/time, never an open "whenever you're ready."

### 7. Thin or stale notes on a second call

**Technique — re-run a short discovery pass live instead of assuming the first rep's notes are enough.**
- **Phil Nykamp** — his own notes from the first call no longer make sense
  to him. Tomás re-asks "what is your goal" from scratch rather than
  plowing ahead, then closes.
- **Lou Barbee** — Sean's handoff note is thin ("wants to share her wealth
  of knowledge"); Tomás gets her to restate the real goal live before
  framing the offer around it.

## Part 3 — Also worth coaching Tomás on (for balance, not just praise)

- **Reba Miller** — too much rapport/backstory relative to call length; the
  two most serious objections raised (cost/ROI, lead-quality skepticism)
  came right as the call ran out of time and were **never actually
  answered**. Lesson: reserve enough time before a known hard stop (school
  pickup, etc.) to handle objections, don't let rapport eat the runway.
- **Stalling objections** (Part 2 §6) — the most consistent gap across the
  whole batch.
- **Chris Twardy** — concedes "I already have a team" without connecting
  how outsourcing still frees the lead's time even when they already have
  help — same category as the Jason Flesher near-miss above.

## Suggested practice format (for the team training session)

1. Read a real lead quote from Part 1 or Part 2 above (the stated goal or
   objection, not Tomás's response) and have the rep respond live, cold.
2. Compare to Tomás's actual line — not to memorize the script, but to
   check the response has the same shape: name the lead's own stated
   goal/problem, map a specific mechanism to it, back it with a number or
   named example.
3. Run the "dead competitor" live-search drill explicitly — have each rep
   practice searching a lead's city/niche and narrating what they find,
   since it's low-effort and repeatable.
4. Close with the stalling drill: give reps an open-ended stall line and
   have them practice converting it into a specific date/time before
   accepting it, using Chad Williams's call as the standard.
